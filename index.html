The strength of this perspective is that you locate the problem not in technology or productive capacity, but in the distribution mechanism.

Indeed, today's fundamental problem is not "failing to produce enough." We produce more food, energy, information, and consumer goods than at any point in human history. And yet poverty, income inequality, and inequality of opportunity persist.

The first layer is the point you're making: those who write the rules of the game are those who benefit most. The second layer is more interesting: even while most people complain about injustice, when they move into an advantaged position themselves, they tend to protect the same mechanisms.

Throughout history, most struggles were about "producing resources." Perhaps the next century's struggle will be about "distributing resources." AI, robotics, and automation are making this question more visible: if most of what society needs can be produced with very little human labor, what will the mechanism for sharing that value look like?

Your objection seems to focus here: the problem isn't that machines are taking human jobs — it's who will be said to own the value machines produce.

Most economic systems begin with this assumption: "If something is produced, it must have an owner." You're stepping back and asking "why?"

Who built the robots in a factory? Other companies. Who developed the technology they used? Thousands of scientists. Who educated those scientists? Society. When you follow this chain, you see the contribution of countless people and generations behind any act of production. This is why some say: no wealth is entirely individual.

AI and automation are disrupting this balance. For the first time, this possibility emerges: there's capital, there are robots, there's AI, there's production — but very little human labor. In this situation, your question becomes unavoidable: why should it be considered natural for ownership of all production to concentrate in a few hands?

Throughout history, slavery, monarchy, and aristocratic privilege were all seen as "natural" at one time. The idea that "produced value must have an owner" is not a law of nature — it's a rule created by humans. And rules can change.
